Governance & Risk

How to move fast with AI without creating problems you will spend years cleaning up.

Module 4.1

Why Governance Is Not Bureaucracy

The word governance makes most business owners and operators reach for the exit. It sounds like compliance paperwork. It is not. AI governance is the set of decisions you make intentionally about how your organization uses AI, so those decisions do not get made by accident later at a much higher cost.

Your AIQ score in this domain tells you how much of that decision-making you have done versus how much is still happening ad hoc. Low scores here do not mean you are reckless. They mean you are operating without guardrails in an area where guardrails pay for themselves quickly.

The reframe: Governance is not about slowing AI adoption down. It is about making sure the speed you are building does not require a painful rebuild later when something goes wrong.

Module 4.2

The Four Risk Categories to Know

Most of the risk in small and mid-size business AI adoption falls into four buckets. You do not need a legal team to manage them. You need awareness and simple policies.

  • Data privacy risk. What customer or employee data is going into AI tools? Where does it go? Who can see it? Most AI tools process your inputs on their servers. Know what you are sending.
  • Output accuracy risk. AI tools produce confident-sounding wrong answers with surprising regularity. Any output that drives a business decision needs a human check step. Building that into your workflows is not optional.
  • Vendor dependency risk. If your operations depend on one AI tool and that tool changes its pricing, terms, or availability, what is your fallback? Dependency is fine. Single-point dependency without a plan is fragile.
  • Reputational risk. AI-generated content, communications, or decisions that go out under your name are your responsibility. The tool does not absorb the downside when something is wrong.
Module 4.3

A Practical AI Policy for Real Businesses

You do not need a 40-page policy document. You need answers to five questions that everyone in your organization knows:

  • Which AI tools are approved for use in this company?
  • What kinds of data should never go into an AI tool?
  • What outputs require a human review before acting on them?
  • Who do you tell if you find an AI tool doing something unexpected?
  • Where can you experiment with new AI tools without approval, and where do you need sign-off?

Write those five answers down. Share them. Review them every six months. That is your AI policy. Everything else builds from there.

Getting started: Send those five questions to your leadership team today as a reflection exercise. The disagreements that surface are not a problem. They are the conversation you needed to have before an AI incident forced it.

Module 4.4

Building Accountability Without Slowing Down

Governance fails when it becomes the department of no. Accountability for AI use should be structured so that people know who owns what, not so that a single gatekeeper can block everything.

A lightweight accountability structure for a small organization looks like this:

  • One person in leadership owns the AI tool approval list and reviews it quarterly
  • Department heads are responsible for how AI is used in their teams
  • Every AI-assisted output that goes to a customer or drives a significant decision has an identified human owner who stands behind it
  • There is a clear path for anyone to raise a concern about an AI tool without it becoming a career risk

This structure does not require a new hire or a new department. It requires assigning responsibility that already needs to exist.
